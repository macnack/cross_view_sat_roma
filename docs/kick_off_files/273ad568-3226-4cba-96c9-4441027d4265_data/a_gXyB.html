Camera-only BEV-to-orthophoto localization from a single spherical camera

Teach a 360° camera the geometry a LiDAR sees during training, project its features into a gravity-aligned bird's-eye view without learned depth lifting, and localize against free public orthophotos with multi-hypothesis consensus.

1. Motivation

Ground robots and vehicles need an absolute position when GNSS is unavailable or degraded. Georeferenced orthophotos are free in many countries (Environment Agency in England, Lantmäteriet, Maanmittauslaitos, national geoportals in Poland) and never drift, but matching a street-level view against a top-down map is hard: the two views share almost no appearance, and the map may be years older than the query.

Existing approaches pick one of three prices. Learned lifting (Lift-Splat, BEV-Patch-PF) predicts a depth distribution per pixel and needs depth or LiDAR at inference for good accuracy. Depth-lifted matching (Loc², BevSplat) depends on monocular depth models that are only reliable up to scale. Flat IPM (G2SWeakly) is cheap but fails far from the camera and on anything above the ground. Our position: with a spherical camera, an IMU and a known camera height, the ground is already geometrically determined; what is missing is where walls meet the ground and which way surfaces face. A LiDAR can teach both during training and is not needed afterwards.

The second ingredient already exists in the group: Sat-RoMa (ACIVS 2026) showed that for cross-temporal overhead matching the correct correspondence often survives only as a secondary mode of the coarse posterior, and that keeping several Gaussian modes per patch and letting geometric consensus pick among them cuts corner error more than seven-fold. Ground-to-map matching has the same repeated-structure ambiguity, so the same delayed-commitment matcher applies.

2. Hypotheses

Each hypothesis is stated with the experiment that can falsify it. A hypothesis that survives becomes a claim in the paper; one that fails becomes a reported finding or a reason to stop.

Dependencies. H1 gates everything. H2 and H3 together decide whether the paper is "geometry is enough" or "closing the gap to LiDAR". H5 is the method contribution; H6 is the headline comparison; H4 and H7 are supporting ablations. BevSplat's evidence against raw IPM means H2 must be tested with the contact line and head, never with raw IPM alone.

3. Method

3.1 Geometric BEV projection

Each ERP pixel defines a ray; the IMU gives roll and pitch, so the ray is expressed in a gravity-aligned frame and intersected with the ground plane at camera height h. This places every ground pixel without a network. For buildings, a predicted wall–ground contact row vc(u) places the footprint, and facade features above it are collapsed onto that cell — the position the orthophoto shows. Elevated content with no ground contact (canopy, signs) is masked. A small per-column ground-height residual absorbs road slope.

3.2 Geometry head taught by LiDAR

A head on the shared image encoder predicts, per ERP patch, a surface normal in the gravity frame, a planarity score, the contact line and a confidence. Labels come from local plane fits to LiDAR points in each ERP window, using the calibrated extrinsics of Dur360BEV. The descriptor (|nz|, cos 2α, sin 2α, planarity, contact, confidence) is embedded by an MLP, gated by validity and added to the projected image feature. Azimuth is doubled so the normal sign is irrelevant, and it rotates with the BEV, so yaw stays an unknown to be estimated.

3.3 Matching and pose

The BEV query and an orthophoto tile of four times its extent go through the Sat-RoMa coarse matcher. For each query patch a Gaussian mixture is fitted to the posterior; the union of modes forms a multi-hypothesis correspondence set; consensus selects the mutually consistent modes. Because the camera height fixes metric scale, the solver is a 2-point SE(2) RANSAC (49 trials at w = 0.3, p = 0.99 versus 567 for a homography); a homography remains the fallback when the ground-plane assumption is violated.

3.4 Training

Three losses on one network. (i) The Sat-RoMa regression-by-classification loss on the coarse posterior, with ground-truth correspondences from the RTK/INS pose. (ii) Orientation-aware contrastive alignment on pre-decoder features: anchor = valid BEV cell, positive = the RTK-projected orthophoto cell (Gaussian-soft over a 3×3 neighbourhood; sampled from a random year when multi-year orthophotos exist), negatives = other cells, with hard negatives from cells within 20 m whose edge orientation matches the anchor's predicted wall azimuth. This is the method's main novelty: it shapes the embedding the matcher correlates over so that repeated same-orientation structure is separated before the posterior is formed. (iii) Cosine + BCE geometry losses against LiDAR labels.

6. Risks

  • Durham orthophoto coverage or licence blocks the cross-temporal experiment — the one dataset advantage.
  • Raw IPM far below the oracle and the head only half-closes the gap; H2 collapses into BevSplat's result.
  • Theta S resolution: DINOv3 at patch 16 gives 80×40 features (~1.6 m of arc at 20 m); use stride-4 ConvNeXt features or sample RGB first.
  • Yaw: Sat-RoMa trained with ±55°; a ground robot needs 360° unless a heading prior is trusted.
  • Non-true orthophotos displace roofs from footprints by Δ = rh/(H−h) (Fig. 6).
  • Novelty perception: reusing Sat-RoMa unchanged invites "application paper"; the contrastive alignment and geometry head must carry the method section.
